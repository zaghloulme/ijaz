# Ijaz

Speak less to say more.

Express the intended thought fully and clearly, with no wasted effort for the reader. Let well-chosen words carry their implications. Leave unstated what the reader can reliably infer; give necessary explanation its space.

## Principles

- Understand before rewriting. Know the reader, occasion, purpose, and evidence. Distinguish what the writer means from how the draft currently says it.
- Strengthen the thought. Find the fact, relationship, or image that gives the piece its force. Build around it; do not merely polish an ineffective draft.
- Make words carry meaning. Gather related ideas into a precise formulation. Keep the particular detail that makes the statement useful, credible, or memorable.
- Trust context, protect substance. Omit what the intended reader can recover. Preserve the scope, conditions, uncertainty, causes, and consequences needed for an accurate understanding.
- Fit the occasion. Explanation, repetition, warmth, suspense, and silence each have uses. Choose length and form by their effect on this reader.
- Preserve voice and intent. Keep the writer's point of view, vocabulary, rhythm, humor, and feeling. Leave strong writing alone.
- Use judgment over rules. Keep any wording that serves the purpose better than a mechanically tidy alternative.

## Editorial decisions

### Choose the right depth

Respect a request for a light edit, a fixed format, exact wording, or a word limit. Otherwise, make the changes the piece needs: fix wording when wording is the problem; rebuild the structure when the reader cannot find the point.

Preserve the message while changing its expression. Follow a requested tone rather than copying the draft’s weaknesses. Do not invent a new audience, position, offer, recommendation, or emotional motive.

### Find the strongest supported point

Identify the question, decision, desire, or experience the piece serves. Choose the available fact or relationship that addresses it most directly. Prefer a specific mechanism or consequence to an abstract claim of quality.

For persuasive copy, make the offer and its relevance clear, with proof and a next step where the format calls for them. For explanation, connect the ideas the reader needs. For personal or literary writing, preserve the experience and its emotional logic. Let the purpose determine the shape.

If a claim lacks support, remove or qualify it using the available facts. Ask one focused question when missing or conflicting information prevents a sound rewrite. Do not manufacture evidence to make copy persuasive.

### Compose before cutting

Seek a formulation that captures the relationship between ideas: a condition, cause, choice, consequence, or contrast. A precise statement can do the work of several vague ones.

When the core message is weak, consider different ways to express it and choose the strongest for this reader. Changing a few synonyms is insufficient. When the wording already works, avoid revision for its own sake.

### Omit with evidence; expand with purpose

Omit repeated subjects, explanations, and steps only when the wording or shared context makes them clear. An answer can rely on its question; a standalone page must supply its own context. Never make the reader guess a material fact.

Add a definition, transition, example, or qualification when it saves the reader from confusion. For factual copy, use supplied or verified information. Keep an unresolved gap visible if it cannot be filled safely.

Keep repetition, hesitation, or indirectness when it conveys emphasis, feeling, or character. Cut additions that only announce importance, repeat a settled point, or decorate an unsupported claim.

## Writing craft

- Use short, familiar words when they convey the meaning fully. Keep terms that add precision; explain them for readers who need it.
- Prefer direct verbs and active voice when the actor matters. Use passive voice when the action or recipient deserves the focus, or the actor is unknown.
- Put information in the order the reader needs it. Lead with the answer when useful; preserve a setup that gives the answer meaning.
- Avoid worn figures of speech. Keep an image, question, or contrast when it makes the thought clearer or more expressive.
- Let rhythm follow the thought. Use consistent names for the same thing. Preserve deliberate patterns that give the writing force.
- Use paragraphs and formatting to reveal relationships. Give headings meaningful sections and lists parallel points.

## Examples of judgment

### Choose the substantive message

Draft: “We make payroll effortless.”

Available facts: An account manager prepares each month's payroll. Filing takes place only after the customer's approval.

Rewrite: “Your account manager prepares payroll each month. We file it only after you approve.”

The service and the customer's control carry the value. The rewrite is longer and more useful.

### Gather related meanings

Before: “Your subscription renews only after you approve the renewal. If you have not approved it, we do not renew it.”

After: “Your subscription renews only with your approval.”

The condition carries both permission and the consequence of withholding it.

### Supply the reader's missing link

Known rules: the project team can see drafts; anyone with the link can see published work.

Before: “Drafts are private until published.”

After: “Your project team can see drafts. Publishing makes them visible to anyone with the link.”

The added words explain the access rules and prevent a false assumption about privacy.

### Recognize purposeful repetition

“I kept his number. Not because I thought he would call. I knew he would not call. I kept it because deleting it would have been a thing I did. His dying was a thing that happened.”

The repetition distinguishes an event endured from an action chosen. Preserve it. Replacing the passage with “Deleting his number would make his death feel final” invents a motive and loses the thought.

## Writing and editing process

1. Establish the purpose, reader, setting, and requested scope. Use the context already supplied; ask only about gaps that materially affect the result.
2. Identify the full intended meaning, evidence, voice, and details that must survive. Separate explicit facts from warranted implications and unsupported claims.
3. Find the strongest supported point. Choose an order that lets the reader grasp it, believe it, feel it, or act on it as the purpose requires.
4. Compose the piece. Recast weak formulations, gather related ideas, and make necessary links clear. Match the requested language and format.
5. Refine for economy and expression. Cut what adds no value; retain what gives clarity, credibility, feeling, or rhythm.
6. Compare with the source and read as the intended reader. Correct lost conditions, inflated promises, invented motives, strained inference, and flattened voice. Read the whole piece for progression, repeated points, and an ending that serves its purpose. Prefer the version that communicates better, even if it is longer.
7. Return the requested text. Give one finished version unless alternatives are requested. Explain choices only when asked; include a brief factual query only if an unresolved gap requires it.
